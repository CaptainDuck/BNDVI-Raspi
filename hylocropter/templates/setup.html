    Step 1 of 9 · Background

    What the blue gel is actually doing

    Your camera is a Pi NoIR — the same sensor as a normal Pi camera, but with the infrared-blocking filter left out. So it sees visible light and near-infrared, which your eyes can't.

    That matters because of one fact about plants: healthy leaves reflect near-infrared very strongly and absorb blue light. A stressed or dying leaf reflects less NIR. So comparing "how much NIR" with "how much blue" tells you something about plant health before you can see anything wrong.

    Yes — the blue square in the box is the Rosco #2007 Raspberry Pi's own announcement of it says: “There's a little square of blue gel in there. What's it for? … Our friend Roscolux #2007 Storaro Blue (that's the blue thing's full name) turns out to be a great example — we buy it on giant reels and the guys at the factory in Wales … cut it up into little squares for you to use.” Same object, two names. You don't need to buy anything.

    The gel's job is to get rid of red light. Here's what it lets through, from Rosco's own data sheet:

    Blue 420–440 nm53% through
    Green 500–560 nm8–18% — mostly stopped
    Red 640–660 nm2% — blocked
    Near-infrared 700–740 nm15% → 67% and rising

    With red gone, the camera's three colour channels stop meaning what their names say:

    {{ kv('The RED channel now holds', 'near-infrared — there is no red left for it to see') }} {{ kv('The GREEN channel holds', 'very little — the gel stopped most of it, and we ignore it') }} {{ kv('The BLUE channel holds', 'visible blue, plus a bit of leaked NIR') }}

    So the number this whole system is built on is just a comparison of two of those channels:

    BNDVI = (NIR − Blue) ÷ (NIR + Blue)  =  (Red channel − Blue channel) ÷ (Red channel + Blue channel)

    It comes out between −1 and +1. Higher means more NIR relative to blue, which means healthier. That's the whole idea — everything after this is making sure the camera measures those two channels honestly.

    Step 3 of 9 · The filter

    Is the blue gel actually in front of the lens?

    This is the single most common way to get meaningless numbers. The gel sits loose inside the clear plastic lens cap, and if the cap pops off it falls out — and nothing complains. The photos still look like photos, and the dashboard still produces a number. It's just wrong.

    Point the camera at leafy plants in daylight, then read the verdict below. Two independent tells:

    • The picture. With the gel on, vegetation looks pink or magenta — that's NIR arriving in the red channel. Without it, the picture looks like an ordinary photo.
    • The green channel. The gel stops most green, so green should be well below blue. Without the gel, green is the brightest channel on foliage. That's what the check measures.

    Mounting it: snap the clear lens cap off, cut the gel slightly smaller than the inside of the cap, drop it in, snap the cap back on. On the drone, tape the cap down too — an F450 vibrates hard enough to shake it loose. Never glue it to the lens. Full detail in HARDWARE.md.

    Step 4 of 9 · Locking the camera

    Why the camera has to stop being clever

    An ordinary camera constantly adjusts itself to make pictures look nice. For this rig, every one of those adjustments is damage — because we are measuring the ratio between two channels, and the camera adjusts those channels separately.

    {{ kv('Auto white balance', 'Re-boosts red and blue independently to make white look white. It would cancel out the exact difference we are measuring.') }} {{ kv('Auto exposure', 'Changes shutter and gain per shot, so two photos of the same plant are not comparable.') }} {{ kv('Sharpening and denoise', 'Non-linear and uneven across the frame.') }} {{ kv('Colour correction', 'Mixes the green channel into red and blue — the channel we said was blocked.') }}

    You don't have to configure any of that: it's already done, and the dashboard checks it on every single frame by comparing what the camera reports doing against what was asked. If a setting is being silently ignored — which is a real bug in some versions — you'll see it here rather than never finding out.

    Checking…
    —

    There is one thing worth doing by hand later, on the rig: point at a fixed scene and shade it with your hand. The picture should get darker while the BNDVI mean barely moves. If the picture doesn't change at all, the lock failed. Step 8 comes back to this.

    Step 5 of 9 · Exposure

    Getting the brightness right

    Two knobs, and they're the only camera settings you'll normally touch:

    {{ kv('Exposure time', 'How long the shutter stays open. Longer = brighter, but more motion blur — which matters on a moving drone.') }} {{ kv('Analogue gain', 'How much the signal is amplified. Higher = brighter, but noisier.') }}

    What you're aiming for: put a sheet of plain white paper in the shot, and adjust until its brightest pixels read 180–230 out of 255. Bright enough to use the sensor's range; not so bright that a channel hits 255 and clips. A clipped channel makes BNDVI read falsely flat, and no maths afterwards can recover it.

    Drag a box over the white paper in the feed below, then press Check. Adjust the sliders and re-check until it says good.

    {# ═══ 6. the k coefficient ══════════════════════════════════════════════ #}
    Step 6 of 9 · The leak

    The blue channel is cheating, and we can measure by how much

    Look back at the transmission table in step 1. The gel passes blue — but it passes near-infrared too, and the blue pixels on the sensor can see NIR. So the "blue" channel is really blue + a bit of NIR. That contamination makes plants look less healthy than they are, because it inflates the number we subtract.

    The fix is to subtract an estimate of it before computing the index:

    real blue ≈ blue channel − (k × NIR)

    All of which comes down to: what is k for your camera? And there's a neat trick. White paper reflects NIR and visible light about equally, so a correctly corrected rig must read BNDVI ≈ 0 on white. Set the formula to give zero and it rearranges to a single answer:

    k = (blue ÷ NIR) − 1

    So the dashboard can just measure it off your white paper. Keep the box from the last step over the paper and press the button.

    Use the box from step 5, then solve.
    —
    Don't trust the number 0.8 You'll see 0.8 quoted for this everywhere, including in older versions of this project's own notes. It's a real value from Public Lab — but for a different filter (a narrowband red one, with the channels the other way round). For the Rosco #2007, expect your measured k to come out well below 0.8. Yours is better than theirs.

    Step 7 of 9 · The three colours

    Where green becomes yellow becomes red

    BNDVI is a continuous number, but the map shows three bands, because "which rows do I walk today" is a three-way decision. These two values are where the colours change.

    Doing well above {{ '%.2f'|format(cfg.threshold_healthy) }}
    Needs a look below {{ '%.2f'|format(cfg.threshold_moderate) }}
    —

    The defaults — 0.30 and 0.10 — match what Public Lab practitioners report for this exact camera-and-gel combination ("healthy plants 0.3 to 0.7, non-plants below 0.2"). They're a reasonable place to start.

    These are not dragon fruit numbers yet Those thresholds come from leafy crops. Hylocereus is a cactus with thick waxy cladodes and will not behave the same way. Deriving your own is your Objective 4 validation, and it's straightforward: photograph plants you've already judged by eye — clearly healthy, clearly cankered, and some in between — read each mean off its detail page, and pick the thresholds that best separate your groups. Every photo keeps the thresholds it was taken with, so changing these later never rewrites history.
    Defaults are fine for now.
